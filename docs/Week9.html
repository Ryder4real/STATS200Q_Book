9  Sufficency

9.1 Background

We have finally finished covering hypothesis testing, and have moved onto our final topic: sufficiency. A sufficient statistic \(T(x_1,...,x_n)\), means that \(T\) contains all useful information with respect to the data. This means that once we have \(T\), we do not care how we got there. As a quick example, let’s say that \(T = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\), and it is a sufficient statistic for \(\theta\). This means that for properly estimating \(\theta\), we do not need to know \(x_1,...,x_n\). In fact, we only need to know \(T\).

Furthermore, for a frequentist application, the Rao-Blackwell theorem says that for any unbiased estimator \(\hat{\theta}\), then \(\hat{\theta}_{RB} = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}(T)]\) is also unbiased and \(\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{RB}) \leq \text{Var}(\hat{\theta})\). So, this means that the best unbiased estimator only references the data with respect to the sufffcient statistics \(T\).

A quick proof is below. First, let’s show that if \(\hat{\theta}\) is unbiased, then so is \(\hat{\theta}_{RB}\).

$$ \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{RB}] &= \mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta} | T]] \\ \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_{RB}] &= \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta} | T] \text{ [NOTE: From the Law of Total Exectation]} \end{aligned}\]

$$ So, this means that the expected value of both estimators is the same, so they have the same bias. Next, let’s show the variance part.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) &= \mathbb{E}[(\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}|T)] + \text{Var}(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}|T]) \text{ [NOTE: From the Law of Total Variance]} \\ \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) &= (\text{value}> 0) + \text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{RB}) \end{aligned} \]

So, we can see \(\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}_{RB}) \leq \text{Var}(\hat{\theta})\).

This gives way to the sufficiency principle, “If you have a sufficient statistic, use it”. The MLE and Posterior Mean are always both functions of a sufficient statistic. In general, our estimators are usually already sufficient statistics, meaning we do not usually have to apply the Rao-Blackwell theorem to improve our estimator (this process is called Rao-Blackwellization).

9.2 Sufficient Statistic

In class, we learned that for data \(X_i,...X_n\) and parameter \(\theta\), a statistic \(T(x_i,...x_n)\) is sufficient if either of the following hold: \

1: \(f_\theta(x_i,...x_n) = g\Big( \theta, T(x_1,...,x_n)\Big) \cdot h(x_1,,...x_n)\),

where:

\(\quad g\Big( \theta, T(x_1,...,x_n)\Big)\) depends on \(\theta\) only through \(T\)

\(\quad h(x_1,,...x_n)\) depends only on \((x_1,,...x_n)\) and not on \(\theta\)

  1. \(P(X_i=x_1,...,X_n=x_n | T = t)\) does not depend on \(\theta\).

For my Negative Binomial dataset, \(p\) is the parameter we are interested in estimating, so \(p\) will be our \(\theta\).

I will show that for my distribution, both definitions hold:

9.2.1 First Proof

The PDF of my negative Binomial Distribution: \(X_1,...,X_n \sim \text{NegBin}(r, p)\) is:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_p(x_1,...,x_n) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \binom{x_i + r- 1}{x_i} p^r (1 - p)^{x_i} \end{aligned} \]

We can simplify this into:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_p(x_1,...,x_n) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \binom{x_i + r- 1}{x_i} p^r (1 - p)^{x_i} \\ &= p^{rn} \prod_{i=1}^n \binom{x_i + r- 1}{x_i} (1 - p)^{x_i} \\ &= p^{rn} \Bigg( \prod_{i=1}^n \binom{x_i + r- 1}{x_i} \Bigg) (1 - p)^{\sum_{i=1}^nx_i} \end{aligned} \]

We can re-write this as:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_p(x_1,...,x_n) &= p^{rn}(1 - p)^{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i} \cdot \prod_{i=1}^n \binom{x_i + r- 1}{x_i} \end{aligned} \]

Now, as we have been doing, let’s assume \(r\) is fixed and \(p\) is the parameter we are interested in estimating.

Here, if we set \(T(X_1,...x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\), then we can rewrite the equation as:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_p(x_1,...,x_n) &= p^{rn}(1 - p)^{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i} \cdot \prod_{i=1}^n \binom{x_i + r- 1}{x_i} \\ &= p^{rn}(1 - p)^{T} \cdot \prod_{i=1}^n \binom{x_i + r- 1}{x_i} \\ &= g\Big( p, T(x_1,...,x_n)\Big) \cdot h(x_1,,...x_n) \end{aligned} \]

where:

  1. \(g\Big( p, T(x_1,...,x_n)\Big) = p^{rn}(1 - p)^{T}\)

  2. \(h(x_1,,...x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n \binom{x_i + r- 1}{x_i}\)

As we were able to write our PDF in terms of \(g\Big(p, T(x_1,...,x_n)\Big) \cdot h(x_1,,...x_n)\), then this means \(T(x_1,...,x_n)\) is a sufficient statistic.

So, for the Negative Binomial distribution, \(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\) is a sufficient statistic.

9.2.2 Second Proof

Similarly, we could have also confirmed this using the other definition we learned in class, that \(P(X_1=x_1,...,X_n=x_n | T = t)\) does not depend on \(p\).

\[ \begin{aligned} P(X_1=x_1,...,Xn=x_n | T=t) = \frac{P(X_1=x_1,...,X_n=x_n, T=t)}{P(T=t)} \end{aligned} \]

\(T\) is the sum of i.i.d. \(\text{NegBin}(r, p)\) random variables. So, \(T\) is a \(\text{NegBin}(nr, p)\) random variable.

So, we get:

\[ \begin{aligned} P(X_1=x_1,...,Xn=x_n | T=t) &= \frac{P(X_1=x_1,...,X_n=x_n, T=t)}{P(T=t)} \\ P(X_1=x_1,...,Xn=x_n | T=t) &= \frac{p^{rn}(1 - p)^{t} \cdot \prod_{i=1}^n \binom{x_i + r - 1}{x_i}}{\binom{t + nr- 1}{t} p^{rn} (1 - p)^{t}} \\ P(X_1=x_1,...,Xn=x_n | T=t) &= \frac{ \prod_{i=1}^n \binom{x_i + r - 1}{x_i}}{\binom{t + nr - 1}{t} } \\ \end{aligned} \]

As we can see, this does not depend on \(p\), proving once again that \(T(x_1,...x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\) is a sufficient statistic.

9.3 Seeing This in My MLE and Psoterior Mean

As I mentioned earlier, the MLE and Posterior Mean are both always functions of a Suffcient Statistic. From Chapter 1, we have that:

\[ \hat{p}_{MLE} = \frac{r}{r + \bar{x}} \\ \hat{p}_{posterior} = \frac{1 + nr}{2 + nr + \sum_{i=1}^n x_i} \]

As \(T(x_1,...x_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i\), we can rewrite our MLE and Posterior Mean as functions of T:

\[ \hat{p}_{MLE} = \frac{r}{r + \frac{T}{n}} \\ \hat{p}_{posterior} = \frac{1 + nr}{2 + nr + T} \]
